Lean Fundamentals

Free daily lessons, days 1–30.

Day 1 · Lean Foundations

What Is Lean?

Deliver what the customer values, with less waste.

Lean is a way of working that delivers what the customer values using less time, effort, space and material. It does this by removing steps that add no value and making work flow.

Key takeaway: Observe the process. Find the gap. Improve the standard.

Day 2 · Lean Foundations

What Is Value?

Value is what the customer is willing to pay for.

Value is defined by the customer: the right product, at the right quality and quantity, delivered at the right time and cost. Work that moves the product toward that need adds value.

Key takeaway: Observe the process. Find the gap. Improve the standard.

Day 3 · Lean Foundations

Value-Added vs Non-Value-Added

Keep the value. Reduce the necessary. Remove the waste.

Value-added (VA) work changes the product in a way the customer wants and is done right the first time. Non-value-added (NVA) work uses time or resources without adding value. Some NVA is necessary today, such as required tests and safety checks: reduce it. The rest is pure waste: eliminate it.

Key takeaway: Observe the process. Find the gap. Improve the standard.

Day 4 · Lean Foundations

The 5 Lean Principles

Five steps that turn customer value into a flowing process.

The 5 Lean principles are: specify value from the customer's view, map the value stream, make the work flow, let the customer pull, and pursue perfection through continuous improvement.

Key takeaway: Value, stream, flow, pull, perfection: in that order.

Day 5 · Lean Foundations

Customer Value

The customer decides what is worth paying for.

Customer value is the benefit a customer receives from a product or service compared with what they give up for it: price, waiting and effort. Improvement starts by understanding that need in the customer's own words.

Key takeaway: Ask the customer. Do not assume what they value.

Day 6 · Lean Foundations

Waste in Manufacturing

Anything that uses resources but adds no customer value.

Waste (muda) is any activity that consumes time, material, space, energy or effort without adding value the customer will pay for. Removing waste lets the same team deliver more value with less effort.

Key takeaway: If it adds no value, question why it exists.

Day 7 · Lean Foundations

The 8 Wastes (DOWNTIME)

Remember DOWNTIME to spot the eight wastes.

The 8 wastes are Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-utilized talent, Transportation, Inventory, Motion and Extra-processing. The first letters spell DOWNTIME, an easy memory aid for waste walks.

Key takeaway: Learn DOWNTIME, then go and find it.

Day 8 · Lean Foundations

Defects

Work that has to be fixed, redone or thrown away.

Defects are products or services that do not meet requirements and need rework, repair, re-inspection or scrap. Every defect consumes material, time and capacity twice and risks reaching the customer.

Key takeaway: Prevent the defect at its source; do not just catch it.

Day 9 · Lean Foundations

Overproduction

Making more, or earlier, than the customer needs.

Overproduction means producing more than is needed, or before it is needed. It is often called the worst waste because it hides problems and creates other wastes: inventory, handling, space and risk of expiry.

Key takeaway: Make what is needed, when it is needed.

Day 10 · Lean Foundations

Waiting

People, machines or material idle, waiting for the next step.

Waiting is any time when people, equipment or material are idle because the next step is not ready: waiting for material, approval, information, a machine or a test result.

Key takeaway: Make waits visible, then remove their cause.

Day 11 · Lean Foundations

Non-Utilized Talent

Ideas, skills and experience that are never used.

Non-utilized talent is the waste of not using people's knowledge, skills and ideas. Operators often know the causes of problems best, but are not asked or not given time to improve.

Key takeaway: The people doing the work hold the best ideas.

Day 12 · Lean Foundations

Transportation

Moving material more than the process needs.

Transportation waste is unnecessary movement of materials, products or information between locations. Every move adds time, cost and risk of damage without changing the product.

Key takeaway: Shorten the route and reduce the moves.

Day 13 · Lean Foundations

Inventory

More material than the process needs right now.

Inventory waste is any raw material, work-in-progress or finished goods beyond what is needed for smooth flow. Excess stock ties up cash and space and hides problems such as unreliable machines or long changeovers.

Key takeaway: Lower the water level to see the rocks.

Day 14 · Lean Foundations

Motion

Movement of people that adds no value.

Motion waste is unnecessary movement by people: walking, reaching, bending, searching or turning. It costs time and effort and can cause strain and injury.

Key takeaway: Bring the work to the person, not the person to the work.

Day 15 · Lean Foundations

Extra-Processing

Doing more than the customer requires.

Extra-processing is any work beyond what the customer needs: extra steps, tighter-than-needed tolerances, duplicate checks or extra approvals and reports.

Key takeaway: Match the process to the real requirement.

Day 16 · Lean Foundations

Waste Walk

A structured walk to see waste where work happens.

A waste walk is a planned visit to the work area to observe the process and note waste using a simple checklist such as DOWNTIME. The goal is to see and record facts, not to blame people.

Key takeaway: Walk, observe, record, then act on the top few.

Day 17 · Lean Foundations

Gemba

The real place where value is created.

Gemba is a Japanese term for the actual place where work happens: the line, the warehouse, the lab. Problems are understood best where they occur, with the people who do the work.

Key takeaway: Go to the place where the work is done.

Day 18 · Lean Foundations

Go and See

Go to the work. See the facts. Show respect.

Go and See (genchi genbutsu) means going to the source to confirm facts with your own eyes before deciding. It replaces assumptions and reports with direct observation.

Key takeaway: Confirm facts at the source before deciding.

Day 19 · Lean Foundations

Lean Thinking

A way of thinking about value, flow and people.

Lean thinking looks at work through the customer's eyes, sees the whole process rather than single departments, and improves step by step with the people doing the work.

Key takeaway: See the whole flow through the customer's eyes.

Day 20 · Lean Foundations

Lean vs Traditional Operations

Two different ways to run a factory.

Traditional operations often push large batches based on forecasts and optimize each department. Lean operations make what customers pull, keep batches small and optimize the whole flow.

Key takeaway: Optimize the flow, not just the machine.

Day 21 · Lean Foundations

Flow

Work moves smoothly from step to step.

Flow means products move from one value-adding step to the next without stopping, waiting or going backwards. Good flow shortens lead time and exposes problems quickly.

Key takeaway: Remove the stops and the work will flow.

Day 22 · Lean Foundations

Pull System

Produce only when the next step signals a need.

A pull system starts or replenishes work only when the downstream process or customer uses something and sends a signal, such as a kanban card or an empty bin.

Key takeaway: Let real demand trigger the work.

Day 23 · Lean Foundations

Perfection Through Kaizen

Perfection is a direction, not a finish line.

Perfection, the fifth Lean principle, means continuously improving toward zero waste through many small steps (kaizen). Each improvement becomes the new standard and the next starting point.

Key takeaway: Each standard is the base for the next improvement.

Day 24 · Lean Foundations

Lean Terminology

Common Lean words every team should know.

Lean uses a shared vocabulary so teams can talk about problems precisely. Key terms include gemba (the real place), muda (waste), kaizen (improvement), takt (demand pace), kanban (signal) and poka-yoke (error-proofing).

Key takeaway: A shared language makes problems easier to discuss.

Day 25 · Lean Foundations

Lean Foundations Review

Value, waste, flow, pull and the people who improve them.

This month covered the core of Lean: value is defined by the customer, waste is everything else, flow and pull connect the process to demand, and kaizen at the gemba keeps improving it.

Key takeaway: Value, waste, flow, pull, people: the Lean foundation.

Day 26 · Lean Foundations

Factory Waste Spotting

Train your eyes to see waste in five minutes.

Waste spotting is the practice of observing one area for a short, fixed time and listing every waste you see. Repeated often, it builds the habit of noticing what has become normal.

Key takeaway: What looks normal is often waste.

Day 27 · Lean Foundations

Value Stream Basics

All the steps from order to delivery.

A value stream is the complete set of activities, value-adding and not, needed to bring a product from customer order or raw material to delivery. Seeing the whole stream shows where time is lost.

Key takeaway: See the whole stream, not just your own step.

Day 28 · Lean Foundations

Lead Time vs Cycle Time

Two times that are easy to confuse.

Cycle time is the time one process step takes to complete one unit. Lead time is the total time from start (such as an order) to delivery, including all waiting. Lead time is usually much longer than the sum of cycle times.

Key takeaway: Cut the waiting to cut the lead time.

Day 29 · Lean Foundations

Process vs Operation

The flow of the product versus the work of the people.

A process is the flow of material or information through steps over time. An operation is the work done by a person or machine at one point. Improving operations alone may not improve the process.

Key takeaway: Improve the process first, then the operations.

Day 30 · Lean Foundations

Lean in Daily Work

Small Lean habits for every shift.

Lean in daily work means building small habits: start the shift with a short huddle, keep the workplace in order, make problems visible, follow the standard, and raise one improvement idea each week.

Key takeaway: Small daily habits beat big occasional projects.
