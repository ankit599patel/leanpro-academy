Lean & Six Sigma Basics

Free daily lessons, days 1–30.

Day 1 · Lean Foundations

What Is Lean?

Deliver what the customer values, with less waste.

Lean is a way of working that delivers what the customer values using less time, effort, space and material. It does this by removing steps that add no value and making work flow.

Key takeaway: Observe the process. Find the gap. Improve the standard.

Day 2 · Lean Foundations

What Is Value?

Value is what the customer is willing to pay for.

Value is defined by the customer: the right product, at the right quality and quantity, delivered at the right time and cost. Work that moves the product toward that need adds value.

Key takeaway: Observe the process. Find the gap. Improve the standard.

Day 3 · Lean Foundations

Value-Added vs Non-Value-Added

Keep the value. Reduce the necessary. Remove the waste.

Value-added (VA) work changes the product in a way the customer wants and is done right the first time. Non-value-added (NVA) work uses time or resources without adding value. Some NVA is necessary today, such as required tests and safety checks: reduce it. The rest is pure waste: eliminate it.

Key takeaway: Observe the process. Find the gap. Improve the standard.

Day 4 · Lean Foundations

The 5 Lean Principles

Five steps that turn customer value into a flowing process.

The 5 Lean principles are: specify value from the customer's view, map the value stream, make the work flow, let the customer pull, and pursue perfection through continuous improvement.

Key takeaway: Value, stream, flow, pull, perfection: in that order.

Day 5 · Lean Foundations

Customer Value

The customer decides what is worth paying for.

Customer value is the benefit a customer receives from a product or service compared with what they give up for it: price, waiting and effort. Improvement starts by understanding that need in the customer's own words.

Key takeaway: Ask the customer. Do not assume what they value.

Day 6 · Lean Foundations

Waste in Manufacturing

Anything that uses resources but adds no customer value.

Waste (muda) is any activity that consumes time, material, space, energy or effort without adding value the customer will pay for. Removing waste lets the same team deliver more value with less effort.

Key takeaway: If it adds no value, question why it exists.

Day 7 · Lean Foundations

The 8 Wastes (DOWNTIME)

Remember DOWNTIME to spot the eight wastes.

The 8 wastes are Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-utilized talent, Transportation, Inventory, Motion and Extra-processing. The first letters spell DOWNTIME, an easy memory aid for waste walks.

Key takeaway: Learn DOWNTIME, then go and find it.

Day 8 · Lean Foundations

Defects

Work that has to be fixed, redone or thrown away.

Defects are products or services that do not meet requirements and need rework, repair, re-inspection or scrap. Every defect consumes material, time and capacity twice and risks reaching the customer.

Key takeaway: Prevent the defect at its source; do not just catch it.

Day 9 · Lean Foundations

Overproduction

Making more, or earlier, than the customer needs.

Overproduction means producing more than is needed, or before it is needed. It is often called the worst waste because it hides problems and creates other wastes: inventory, handling, space and risk of expiry.

Key takeaway: Make what is needed, when it is needed.

Day 10 · Lean Foundations

Waiting

People, machines or material idle, waiting for the next step.

Waiting is any time when people, equipment or material are idle because the next step is not ready: waiting for material, approval, information, a machine or a test result.

Key takeaway: Make waits visible, then remove their cause.

Day 11 · Lean Foundations

Non-Utilized Talent

Ideas, skills and experience that are never used.

Non-utilized talent is the waste of not using people's knowledge, skills and ideas. Operators often know the causes of problems best, but are not asked or not given time to improve.

Key takeaway: The people doing the work hold the best ideas.

Day 12 · Lean Foundations

Transportation

Moving material more than the process needs.

Transportation waste is unnecessary movement of materials, products or information between locations. Every move adds time, cost and risk of damage without changing the product.

Key takeaway: Shorten the route and reduce the moves.

Day 13 · Lean Foundations

Inventory

More material than the process needs right now.

Inventory waste is any raw material, work-in-progress or finished goods beyond what is needed for smooth flow. Excess stock ties up cash and space and hides problems such as unreliable machines or long changeovers.

Key takeaway: Lower the water level to see the rocks.

Day 14 · Lean Foundations

Motion

Movement of people that adds no value.

Motion waste is unnecessary movement by people: walking, reaching, bending, searching or turning. It costs time and effort and can cause strain and injury.

Key takeaway: Bring the work to the person, not the person to the work.

Day 15 · Lean Foundations

Extra-Processing

Doing more than the customer requires.

Extra-processing is any work beyond what the customer needs: extra steps, tighter-than-needed tolerances, duplicate checks or extra approvals and reports.

Key takeaway: Match the process to the real requirement.

Day 16 · Introduction to Lean Six Sigma

What is Six Sigma?

Reduce variation so every output meets the requirement.

Six Sigma is a data-driven approach to improving processes by reducing variation and defects. It uses the DMAIC method and statistical tools to find and control the causes of variation.

Key takeaway: Less variation means fewer defects.

Day 17 · Introduction to Lean Six Sigma

Lean vs Six Sigma Difference

Two approaches with different strengths.

Lean targets waste and lead time using tools like VSM, 5S and kanban. Six Sigma targets variation and defects using data and statistics through DMAIC. Choose by the type of problem.

Key takeaway: Waste problem? Lean. Variation problem? Six Sigma.

Day 18 · Introduction to Lean Six Sigma

Why Combine Them?

Lean removes waste; Six Sigma removes variation. Together they do both.

Lean makes work flow by removing steps that add no value. Six Sigma makes each step reliable by reducing variation. Combined, they give processes that are both fast and right first time, which neither method achieves fully on its own.

Key takeaway: Speed without quality is waste; quality without speed is cost.

Day 19 · Introduction to Lean Six Sigma

DPMO & Sigma Level Basics

Count defects per million chances to compare any process.

DPMO is defects divided by (units × opportunities per unit), multiplied by one million. It lets you compare simple and complex processes on one scale. A DPMO of about 3.4 corresponds to six sigma, while about 6,210 corresponds to four sigma.

Key takeaway: Measure defects per opportunity, not just per unit.

Day 20 · Introduction to Lean Six Sigma

Common Misunderstandings & Why Leaders Need Lean Six Sigma

Lean Six Sigma is not headcount cutting, and it is not only for experts.

Common myths are that Lean means fewer people, that Six Sigma needs heavy statistics for every problem, and that it is a project for the quality department. Leaders need it because it gives a common language, facts for decisions and a way to build problem solvers at every level.

Key takeaway: Leaders set the example; tools follow behaviour.

Day 21 · Introduction to Lean Six Sigma

Gemba: Go and See

Real answers are found where the work is done.

Gemba means the real place where value is created: the line, the store, the lab. Going to see means watching the process, asking open questions and checking facts before deciding. Reports describe a problem; the gemba shows it.

Key takeaway: Go see, ask why, show respect.

Day 22 · Waste & Variation

Muda, Mura, Muri

Waste, unevenness and overburden are linked.

Muda is waste, Mura is unevenness in flow or workload, and Muri is overburden on people or machines. Uneven schedules create overburden, and overburden creates waste such as defects and breakdowns. Levelling the work often removes all three together.

Key takeaway: Fix the unevenness and much of the waste goes with it.

Day 23 · Waste & Variation

Variation Concept

No two outputs are exactly the same.

Variation is the natural difference between outputs of the same process. Common-cause variation is built into the process; special-cause variation comes from a specific, unusual event and should be investigated.

Key takeaway: Know which variation you are looking at before acting.

Day 24 · Waste & Variation

Types of Variation

Common cause is the system; special cause is an event.

Common cause variation is the normal, steady noise built into the process: material, method, machine and environment. Special cause variation comes from a specific event such as a wrong setting or a new supplier lot. Treat special causes one by one; reduce common causes by changing the system.

Key takeaway: Do not react to noise; do act on signals.

Day 25 · Waste & Variation

Waste in a Real Plant: Example

In a filling and packing area, a waste walk often finds operators waiting for empty bottles, cartons carried long distances, rework of labels and extra stock at the line. Each finding is counted, photographed and given an owner. Fixing the top three usually frees hours of time every shift.

Key takeaway: Count the waste you see, then fix the biggest first.

Day 26 · Waste & Variation

Mistakes in Identifying Waste & Waste Elimination Strategy

Waste is often hidden in "normal" work.

Typical mistakes are calling busy people productive, ignoring office and information waste, and looking only for defects. A good strategy is to walk the process, list waste by type, measure the biggest items, remove the cause rather than the symptom and then standardize the new way.

Key takeaway: See it, measure it, remove the cause, standardize.

Day 27 · Waste & Variation

Lean Thinking vs Traditional Operations

Traditional pushes work through; Lean pulls value to the customer.

Traditional operations focus on keeping machines busy, large batches and inspection at the end. Lean thinking focuses on the customer, flow, small batches, quality at source and people solving problems every day. The shift is in mindset more than in tools.

Key takeaway: Think flow and value, not just utilization.

Day 28 · DMAIC Overview

What is DMAIC?

Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control.

DMAIC is the five-phase Six Sigma method for improving an existing process: Define the problem, Measure the current performance, Analyze causes, Improve with tested solutions, and Control to sustain the gain.

Key takeaway: Follow the phases; do not skip to solutions.

Day 29 · DMAIC Overview

Define Phase

Agree the problem, scope and goal first.

The Define phase clarifies the problem, the customer and their requirements, the project scope, the goal and the team. Key outputs are a project charter, a SIPOC and the voice of the customer.

Key takeaway: A well-defined problem is half solved.

Day 30 · DMAIC Overview

Measure Phase

Understand current performance with reliable data.

The Measure phase establishes how the process performs today. The team defines measures, checks that the measurement system is reliable, collects baseline data and maps the process in detail.

Key takeaway: Trust the measurement before you trust the data.
