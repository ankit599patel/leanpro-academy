Cost, Quality & Reliability

Free daily lessons, days 271–300.

Day 271 · Strategic Cost Reduction

Waste Cost Mapping

Put a money value on each waste.

Waste cost mapping estimates the cost of each type of waste, such as scrap, waiting, overtime and excess stock, along the value stream. It shows where improvement will save most.

Key takeaway: Waste has a price tag.

Day 272 · Strategic Cost Reduction

Budget Alignment

Link improvement plans to the budget.

Budget alignment builds expected savings from improvement projects into the annual budget and tracks them monthly, so Lean results are visible in financial terms.

Key takeaway: Put the savings in the budget.

Day 273 · Strategic Cost Reduction

Cost Reduction Case

Mapping waste costs on a packing line shows that rework of labels costs more than expected. A label check at the start of each roll removes most of the rework and the cost.

Key takeaway: Measure the cost, then remove the cause.

Day 274 · Strategic Cost Reduction

Short Term Cost Cut Trap & Sustainable Profit Strategy

Quick cuts often cost more later.

Short-term cuts such as stopping maintenance or training save money now but raise breakdowns and defects later. A sustainable profit strategy removes waste and builds capability.

Key takeaway: Cut waste, not the future.

Day 275 · Strategic Cost Reduction

Spare Parts Strategy

The right spares, in the right quantity.

A spare parts strategy decides which parts to stock, how many and where, based on equipment criticality, lead time, failure rate and cost, balancing availability against inventory.

Key takeaway: Stock for criticality and lead time, not for comfort.

Day 276 · Productivity Engineering

Productivity Formula

Output divided by input.

Productivity is output divided by input, such as units per labour hour or tonnes per machine hour. Choose measures that reflect value delivered, not just activity.

Key takeaway: Measure value per hour, not effort.

Day 277 · Productivity Engineering

Man Machine Ratio

See how people and machines share time.

A man-machine chart shows, on a timeline, when the operator and the machine are working, waiting or idle. It helps find waiting time and better ways to combine tasks.

Key takeaway: Use machine time to do useful operator work.

Day 278 · Productivity Engineering

Capacity Planning

Can we make what customers want?

Capacity is the maximum output a process can produce in a period. Comparing capacity with demand shows whether the plant can meet orders, has spare capacity or must add shifts, improve OEE or invest.

Key takeaway: Know your real capacity, not the nameplate.

Day 279 · Productivity Engineering

Utilization vs Efficiency

Busy is not the same as productive.

Productivity is useful output per unit of input. Utilization is how much of the available time a resource is busy. High utilization of a non-constraint can create excess inventory without more sales.

Key takeaway: Measure output that customers need, not activity.

Day 280 · Productivity Engineering

Productivity Case

A packing team measures output per labour hour. Removing walking, balancing work and fixing a frequent jam raises productivity significantly without anyone working harder.

Key takeaway: Remove waste, not effort.

Day 281 · Productivity Engineering

Overburdening & Growth Strategy

Overburden damages people and machines.

Overburdening means pushing people or equipment beyond their capability, which leads to fatigue, errors and breakdowns. Sustainable growth increases capacity by removing waste, not by overloading.

Key takeaway: Grow by removing waste, not by overloading.

Day 282 · Productivity Engineering

Spaghetti Diagram & Ergonomics

Map the walking and fit the work to the person.

A spaghetti diagram draws the path of a person or material on a layout to show unnecessary movement. Ergonomics designs the workplace to reduce bending, reaching and lifting, improving safety and productivity.

Key takeaway: Shorter paths, safer postures.

Day 283 · Zero Defect Strategy

Zero Defect Concept

Aim for no defects, not acceptable defects.

Zero defects is a mindset that every defect can be prevented. It focuses on doing work right the first time through good design, training, standards and mistake proofing.

Key takeaway: Every defect has a cause that can be removed.

Day 284 · Zero Defect Strategy

Prevention System

Find defects late, or stop them from happening.

Inspection detects defects after they are made. Prevention stops defects from being made, through robust process design, standards, error-proofing and control at the source.

Key takeaway: Prevention costs less than detection.

Day 285 · Zero Defect Strategy

Mistake Proofing

Make it impossible to do it wrong.

Mistake proofing (Poka-Yoke) designs processes so errors cannot happen or are detected immediately, for example a fixture that fits only one way or a sensor that stops the line if a cap is missing.

Key takeaway: Design out the error.

Day 286 · Zero Defect Strategy

Standardization

Standards make good results repeatable.

Standardization documents the best known way to do the work, trains everyone and audits it. Without standards, quality depends on who is working today.

Key takeaway: No standard, no improvement.

Day 287 · Zero Defect Strategy

Zero Defect Case

A leaking-bottle problem is solved by a torque sensor that rejects loose caps automatically and a standard check at each shift start. Leak complaints drop to zero for several months.

Key takeaway: Detect and prevent at source.

Day 288 · Zero Defect Strategy

Firefighting Culture & Quality Vision

Firefighting solves today's problem; prevention solves tomorrow's.

In a firefighting culture, the same problems return every week. A quality vision rewards prevention, root cause solving and stable processes.

Key takeaway: Stop fighting fires; remove the fuel.

Day 289 · Zero Defect Strategy

Quality at Source & Quality Alert

Build quality into each step and warn fast.

Quality at source means each operator checks their own work and stops when something is wrong. A quality alert quickly informs everyone about a quality risk, what to check and what to do.

Key takeaway: Check at the step, alert at once.

Day 290 · Reliability Engineering

MTBF

Average time between failures.

Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF) is the average running time between one failure and the next. A higher MTBF means more reliable equipment.

Key takeaway: Track failures to see reliability improve.

Day 291 · Reliability Engineering

MTTR

Average time to repair a failure.

Mean Time To Repair (MTTR) is the average time needed to restore equipment after a failure, including diagnosis, waiting for parts and repair. A lower MTTR means faster recovery.

Key takeaway: Spares, skills and standards cut repair time.

Day 292 · Reliability Engineering

Failure Pattern

Failures follow patterns over the life of equipment.

Failure patterns include early-life failures, random failures and wear-out failures. Knowing the pattern helps choose the right strategy: better installation, condition monitoring or planned replacement.

Key takeaway: Know the pattern, choose the strategy.

Day 293 · Reliability Engineering

Reliability Improvement

Make equipment fail less often.

Reliability improvement analyses repeat failures, removes their root causes, improves design and maintenance and tracks MTBF to confirm results.

Key takeaway: Fewer failures, more uptime.

Day 294 · Reliability Engineering

Reliability Case

A pump fails every few weeks. Analysis finds misalignment and wrong lubrication. Laser alignment and a lubrication standard extend the time between failures many times over.

Key takeaway: Fix the cause and MTBF rises.

Day 295 · Reliability Engineering

Reactive Maintenance & Reliability Leadership

Running to failure is the most expensive strategy.

Reactive maintenance waits for breakdowns, causing unplanned downtime and rushed repairs. Reliability leaders invest in planned and predictive maintenance and track reliability metrics.

Key takeaway: Plan maintenance, do not wait for failure.

Day 296 · Reliability Engineering

Maintenance Backlog

Work waiting to be done.

Maintenance backlog is the amount of approved maintenance work not yet completed, often expressed in hours or weeks of available labour. A growing backlog signals that capacity or planning needs attention.

Key takeaway: Watch the backlog trend, not just the size.

Day 297 · Lean in Office Processes

Office Waste

Waste exists in offices too.

Office waste includes waiting for approvals, searching for files, re-entering data, unnecessary reports and errors in documents. The same Lean thinking used on the shop floor removes it.

Key takeaway: Paper and data can be waste too.

Day 298 · Lean in Office Processes

Process Digitization

Digitize to remove steps, not to copy paper.

Process digitization moves forms and approvals into simple digital systems. First remove unnecessary steps, then digitize the improved process.

Key takeaway: Simplify first, then digitize.

Day 299 · Lean in Office Processes

Approval Cycle Reduction

Fewer approvals, faster decisions.

Long approval cycles delay purchases, payments and changes. Reduce them by setting approval limits, removing duplicate signatures and setting time standards for each step.

Key takeaway: Every signature should add value.

Day 300 · Lean in Office Processes

Workflow Mapping

Map the office process step by step.

Workflow mapping shows each step, handoff and waiting time in an office process, such as purchase requests. It reveals delays and rework so they can be removed.

Key takeaway: See the flow, then fix it.
