Day 271 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Inventory Fundamentals

Stock that serves flow, not stock that hides problems.

Inventory is material held for future use or sale: raw materials, work in progress and finished goods. It protects against variation, but too much ties up cash and space and hides problems.

Key takeaway: Hold enough to protect flow, no more.

Day 272 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Raw Material vs WIP vs FG

Three types of inventory along the stream.

Raw material is purchased input not yet used. Work in progress (WIP) is partly processed material. Finished goods (FG) are completed products ready for sale. Each type has different causes and controls.

Key takeaway: Know which type of stock is growing, and why.

Day 273 · Supply Chain & Inventory

ABC Analysis

Focus control on the items that matter most by value.

ABC analysis classifies inventory items by their share of annual consumption value: A items (few items, high value), B items (moderate) and C items (many items, low value), so control effort matches importance.

Key takeaway: Tight control for A, simple control for C.

Day 274 · Supply Chain & Inventory

XYZ Analysis

Classify items by how predictable demand is.

XYZ analysis classifies items by demand variability: X items have steady demand, Y items vary somewhat, and Z items are irregular. Combined with ABC, it guides planning and stocking policy.

Key takeaway: Steady demand: pull. Erratic demand: plan or make to order.

Day 275 · Supply Chain & Inventory

FIFO

Use the oldest stock first.

FIFO (first in, first out) means using or shipping material in the order it was received or made, so older stock does not age in the store.

Key takeaway: Design the storage so FIFO happens naturally.

Day 276 · Supply Chain & Inventory

FEFO

Use the stock that expires first.

FEFO (first expired, first out) means issuing material with the earliest expiry date first, which matters for products and materials with a shelf life.

Key takeaway: With shelf life, expiry date beats arrival date.

Day 277 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Reorder Point

When to order again.

The reorder point is the stock level at which a new order should be placed. A common form: reorder point = demand during lead time + safety stock.

Key takeaway: Order when stock reaches the trigger, not when it runs out.

Day 278 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Safety Stock

A buffer for uncertainty.

Safety stock is extra inventory held to protect against variation in demand and supply lead time. Its size depends on how much variation exists and the service level wanted.

Key takeaway: Reduce variation to reduce safety stock.

Day 279 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Economic Order Quantity

Balance ordering cost against holding cost.

Economic order quantity (EOQ) is a classic model for the order size that minimizes the total of ordering and holding costs under steady demand: EOQ = √(2 × annual demand × cost per order ÷ holding cost per unit per year).

Key takeaway: Use EOQ as a guide, then apply real constraints.

Day 280 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Inventory Accuracy

System stock should match physical stock.

Inventory accuracy measures how closely the recorded inventory matches the actual physical quantity and location. Poor accuracy causes stockouts, excess orders and planning errors.

Key takeaway: A plan is only as good as the stock record.

Day 281 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Cycle Counting

Count a little often instead of everything once.

Cycle counting counts a small portion of inventory on a regular schedule, often more frequently for A items, so errors are found and their causes fixed throughout the year.

Key takeaway: Count often, fix the cause of every difference.

Day 282 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Stockout vs Overstock

Two opposite problems with one root: poor flow.

A stockout is when an item is not available when needed. Overstock is holding far more than needed. Both often come from poor forecasting, long lead times, large batches and inaccurate records.

Key takeaway: Replenish by consumption to avoid both.

Day 283 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Supplier Quality

Good materials in, good products out.

Supplier quality means ensuring purchased materials meet requirements consistently, through clear specifications, supplier selection, incoming checks, performance monitoring and joint problem solving.

Key takeaway: Specify clearly, measure regularly, improve together.

Day 284 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Supplier Lead Time

The time from order to receipt.

Supplier lead time is the time from placing an order to receiving the material. Its length and variability directly affect reorder points and safety stock.

Key takeaway: Reliable lead time matters as much as short lead time.

Day 285 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Material Readiness

Everything ready before production starts.

Material readiness means confirming, before a production run, that all required materials are available in the right quantity, quality status and location.

Key takeaway: Check readiness before the line is scheduled to start.

Day 286 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Shortage Management

Act early when material will run short.

Shortage management detects potential material shortages early, prioritizes the orders affected, and coordinates actions such as expediting, substitutions (where approved) or re-sequencing the plan.

Key takeaway: See shortages days ahead, not at the line.

Day 287 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Warehouse Slotting

Put each item in the best location.

Warehouse slotting assigns storage locations based on how often items are picked, their size and weight, and safety needs, so picking is fast and safe.

Key takeaway: Fast movers close and low.

Day 288 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Receiving Inspection

Check what arrives before it is used.

Receiving inspection checks incoming materials against the purchase order and specification: identity, quantity, condition, labelling, documents and, where required, sampling for testing.

Key takeaway: Quarantine until released.

Day 289 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Packaging Material Control

The right label, carton and cap, every time.

Packaging material control ensures that printed and unprinted packaging materials are correct, approved, counted, stored securely and reconciled, to prevent mix-ups and mislabelling.

Key takeaway: Count it out, count it back.

Day 290 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Batch Traceability

Trace every batch backward and forward.

Batch traceability means being able to trace a finished batch back to its materials, equipment and records, and forward to the customers who received it.

Key takeaway: Record it at each step so you can trace it later.

Day 291 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Lot Control

Keep each lot identified and separate.

Lot control identifies each lot or batch of material or product and keeps it separated and labelled, so status, quality and use can be tracked and recalls limited.

Key takeaway: Never mix lots without a record.

Day 292 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Dispatch Readiness

Everything checked before the truck leaves.

Dispatch readiness confirms that the order is complete, correctly labelled, documented and loaded safely before shipment.

Key takeaway: Check the order before it leaves, not after a complaint.

Day 293 · Supply Chain & Inventory

OTIF

On time, in full: the customer's view of delivery.

OTIF measures the share of orders delivered on time and in full, as agreed with the customer. An order counts only if both conditions are met.

Key takeaway: Late or short both count as a miss.

Day 294 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Forecast vs Demand

What we predicted versus what customers ordered.

A forecast is an estimate of future demand; actual demand is what customers order. Tracking forecast error helps improve planning and set the right stock buffers.

Key takeaway: Measure forecast error to improve it.

Day 295 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Bullwhip Effect

Small changes at the customer, big swings upstream.

The bullwhip effect is the amplification of demand variability as orders move upstream in a supply chain, caused by batching, forecasting on orders rather than consumption, and delays.

Key takeaway: Share real demand to calm the whip.

Day 296 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Kanban Replenishment

Replenish by consumption signals.

Kanban replenishment refills materials only when a signal shows they have been used, keeping stock within set limits and linking supply to actual consumption.

Key takeaway: Use one bin, refill one bin.

Day 297 · Supply Chain & Inventory

An illustrative case of excess stock.

An inventory waste case shows how excess stock built up, what it cost and how it was reduced. This example is illustrative; use your own data for real cases.

Key takeaway: Cheap to buy can be expensive to hold.

Day 298 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Warehouse 5S

An organized warehouse is safer and faster.

Warehouse 5S organizes storage areas with clear locations, labels, marked aisles, removed obsolete stock and regular cleaning, so picking is accurate and safe.

Key takeaway: Every pallet has a home.

Day 299 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Supply Chain Case Study

An illustrative supply chain improvement.

A supply chain case study connects demand, planning, inventory and supplier performance. This example is illustrative; use your own data for real cases.

Key takeaway: Fix the data and the agreement, then the flow follows.

Day 300 · Supply Chain & Inventory

Inventory Review

Right material, right place, right time.

Inventory review: know your stock types, classify with ABC and XYZ, use FIFO or FEFO, set reorder points and safety stock, keep records accurate, and measure delivery with OTIF.

Key takeaway: Classify, control, count, deliver.
