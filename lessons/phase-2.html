365 Days of Lean · Days 31–60

DMAIC: Define to Improve

Free daily lessons, days 31–60.

Day 31 · DMAIC Overview

Analyze Phase

Find and verify the causes that drive the problem.

The Analyze phase identifies possible causes, then uses data and tests to verify which ones actually drive the problem. Tools include fishbone, 5 Whys, Pareto, scatter plots and hypothesis tests.

Key takeaway: Verify causes with data before improving.

Day 32 · DMAIC Overview

Improve & Control

Improve finds the fix; Control keeps it.

In the Improve phase the team generates solutions to verified root causes, tests them in a pilot and confirms the result with data. In the Control phase the new method is written into standards, monitored with charts and handed to the process owner.

Key takeaway: A gain that is not controlled will slip back.

Day 33 · DMAIC Overview

Why DMAIC Fails & DMAIC Mindset

Projects fail from weak scope, weak data or weak follow-up.

DMAIC fails when the problem is too broad, data is skipped, the team jumps to solutions or no one owns the result after the project. The DMAIC mindset is disciplined curiosity: define clearly, measure before judging, prove causes and lock in gains.

Key takeaway: Discipline in each phase beats speed through them.

Day 34 · DMAIC Overview

Six Sigma Roles & Belts

Clear roles make projects move.

The sponsor or champion owns the business need and removes obstacles. Black Belts lead complex projects and coach others; Green Belts lead projects in their own area part-time; Yellow Belts support with basic tools. The process owner keeps the gains after the project ends.

Key takeaway: Every project needs a sponsor, a leader and an owner.

Day 35 · Define Phase Deep Dive

Problem Statement

Describe the problem with facts, not solutions.

A problem statement describes what is wrong, where, when and how much, compared with the standard or expectation. It does not include causes or solutions.

Key takeaway: State the gap. Leave the solution for later.

Day 36 · Define Phase Deep Dive

Project Charter

The agreement that launches a project.

A project charter documents the business case, problem statement, goal, scope, team, timeline and sponsor. It aligns the team and management before work begins.

Key takeaway: No charter, no shared direction.

Day 37 · Define Phase Deep Dive

SIPOC

Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers.

SIPOC is a high-level map of a process that lists Suppliers, Inputs, the main Process steps, Outputs and Customers. It sets the project boundaries and shows who is involved.

Key takeaway: Agree the boundaries before diving into details.

Day 38 · Define Phase Deep Dive

Voice of Customer (VOC)

What the customer says they need, in their words.

Voice of the Customer (VOC) is the collection of customer needs, expectations and complaints, gathered through interviews, surveys, complaints and feedback, and translated into measurable requirements.

Key takeaway: Listen first, then translate into measures.

Day 39 · Define Phase Deep Dive

CTQ (Critical to Quality)

From a broad need to measurable requirements.

A CTQ (critical-to-quality) tree breaks a broad customer need into specific drivers and then into measurable requirements with targets or limits.

Key takeaway: Every CTQ must be measurable.

Day 40 · Define Phase Deep Dive

Define Phase Mistakes & Strategic Project Selection

Choose the right project, then define it tightly.

Common Define mistakes are a scope too wide, a solution written into the problem statement and no baseline. Strategic selection links projects to business goals, chooses problems with measurable impact and keeps the number of active projects small enough to finish.

Key takeaway: Pick fewer projects and finish them.

Day 41 · Define Phase Deep Dive

DMAIC Project Charter Example

A one-page charter for a DMAIC project.

A DMAIC project charter example shows the business case, problem statement, goal, scope, team and timeline on one page. Illustrative case for learning; replace with your own measured data.

Key takeaway: Agree it on one page before starting.

Day 42 · Measure Phase

Data Collection Plan

Plan what, how, when and who before collecting.

A data collection plan specifies what data to collect, the operational definitions, the sample size and frequency, who collects it, how it is recorded and how it will be used.

Key takeaway: Plan the data before you collect it.

Day 43 · Measure Phase

Types of Data

Continuous data measures; attribute data counts.

Continuous data, such as weight, temperature or time, can take any value and shows small changes. Attribute data, such as pass/fail or defect counts, is easier to collect but needs larger samples. Choose the type before collecting, because it decides which charts and tests you can use.

Key takeaway: Choose your data type before you collect.

Day 44 · Measure Phase

Check Sheet

Collect data simply, at the source.

A check sheet is a simple form for recording data as it happens, usually with tally marks by category, time or location. It makes patterns visible and data easy to summarize.

Key takeaway: Easy to fill means more reliable data.

Day 45 · Measure Phase

Basic Statistics (Mean, Range)

The mean shows the centre; the range shows the spread.

The mean is the sum of values divided by their number. The range is the largest value minus the smallest. Together they give a quick picture of a process: for example, average fill weight and how far fills vary within a sample.

Key takeaway: Always report centre and spread together.

Day 46 · Measure Phase

Measurement System Awareness

Is the measurement itself reliable?

A measurement system analysis (MSA) checks how much of the observed variation comes from the measurement system itself, through studies of repeatability, reproducibility, bias and stability.

Key takeaway: Fix the gauge before blaming the process.

Day 47 · Measure Phase

Poor Data Collection Errors & Data Driven Culture

Bad data leads to bad decisions.

Common errors are unclear definitions, missing records filled in later, rounding and samples taken only when it is convenient. A data-driven culture makes data easy to record at source, checks it regularly and uses it openly in daily meetings.

Key takeaway: Trust in decisions starts with trust in data.

Day 48 · Measure Phase

Operational Definition

Everyone measures the same thing the same way.

An operational definition describes exactly what to measure and how, including the method, the unit, the conditions and what counts as a defect, so that different people get the same result.

Key takeaway: Define it so two people get the same answer.

Day 49 · Analyze Phase

Root Cause vs Symptom

Look past the symptom to what really caused it.

Root cause thinking means asking what condition allowed a problem to happen, and verifying it with evidence, instead of reacting only to the visible symptom.

Key takeaway: Treat causes, not symptoms.

Day 50 · Analyze Phase

5 Why

Ask why until you reach a cause you can act on.

5 Whys is a simple method of asking 'why?' repeatedly, each answer based on facts, until a root cause is found that can be fixed. The number five is a guide, not a rule.

Key takeaway: Each 'why' must be backed by evidence.

Day 51 · Analyze Phase

Fishbone Diagram

Organize possible causes before jumping to answers.

A fishbone (Ishikawa or cause-and-effect) diagram groups possible causes of a problem into categories such as Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement and Environment, so the team considers all angles.

Key takeaway: List causes widely, then verify the few that matter.

Day 52 · Analyze Phase

Pareto Analysis

Focus on the vital few causes.

A Pareto analysis sorts categories from largest to smallest and adds a cumulative line. Usually a few categories create most of the problem. Start root cause work on the tallest bar, then redraw the chart after action to confirm it shrank.

Key takeaway: Attack the biggest bar first.

Day 53 · Analyze Phase

Hypothesis Thinking

State a possible cause, then test it with data.

Hypothesis thinking turns opinions into testable statements, such as "fill weight varies more on night shift". Collect data to confirm or reject it before acting. This prevents teams from spending effort on causes that are not real.

Key takeaway: Test the idea before you trust it.

Day 54 · Analyze Phase

Wrong Root Cause Trap & Analytical Leadership

The first cause found is rarely the root cause.

Teams fall into the trap of blaming people, stopping at the first "why" or choosing the cause that is easiest to fix. Analytical leaders ask for evidence, check that the cause explains the data and confirm by turning the cause on and off where possible.

Key takeaway: Prove the cause before you fix it.

Day 55 · Analyze Phase

Cause-and-Effect Matrix & Affinity Diagram

Rank causes and group ideas before deciding.

A cause-and-effect matrix scores each input against its effect on the key outputs, so the team focuses on the inputs that matter most. An affinity diagram groups many brainstormed ideas into themes, making large lists easier to act on.

Key takeaway: Sort and score before you choose.

Day 56 · Improve Phase

Brainstorming

Rules that bring out more and better ideas.

Good brainstorming sets clear rules: one topic, quantity before quality, no criticism during idea generation, build on others' ideas, and everyone gets a turn. Evaluation comes afterwards.

Key takeaway: Generate first, judge later.

Day 57 · Improve Phase

Kaizen

Change for the better, a little every day.

Kaizen means continuous improvement through small, practical changes made by the people who do the work. Many small improvements, standardized and repeated, add up over time.

Key takeaway: Small changes, made every day, by everyone.

Day 58 · Improve Phase

Poka Yoke

Make mistakes impossible, or instantly visible.

Poka-yoke (error-proofing) is a design feature or device that prevents a mistake from happening or makes it immediately obvious, so a defect is not produced or passed on.

Key takeaway: Design the process so it is hard to get wrong.

Day 59 · Improve Phase

SMED Concept

Single-Minute Exchange of Die: faster changeovers.

SMED is a method for reducing changeover time by separating internal setup (done while stopped) from external setup (done while running), converting internal to external, and streamlining both.

Key takeaway: Do as much as possible while the machine is still running.

Day 60 · Improve Phase

Pilot Implementation

Test on a small scale before full rollout.

A pilot run tests a solution under real conditions on a limited scale, such as one line or one shift, to confirm it works, find side effects and refine it before full implementation.

Key takeaway: Small pilots prevent big mistakes.
