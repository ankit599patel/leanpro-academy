Control, Statistics & OEE

Free daily lessons, days 61–90.

Day 61 · Improve Phase

Improve Phase Risks & Sustainable Improvement Thinking

Test changes for side effects before full rollout.

Improvements can create new risks: new defects, safety hazards or extra workload elsewhere. Run a small pilot, check risks with a simple FMEA and involve operators. Sustainable improvement is simple, standardized and owned by the people who do the work.

Key takeaway: A fix that people cannot sustain is not a fix.

Day 62 · Improve Phase

Solution Selection

Choose solutions with clear criteria.

Solution selection compares options against agreed criteria, such as effect on the root cause, cost, time, risk and ease of implementation, often using a simple weighted matrix.

Key takeaway: Decide with criteria, not with opinions.

Day 63 · Control Phase

Control Plan

One document for how the process stays in control.

A control plan lists, for each key process step, what is measured, the specification, the method, the sample size and frequency, who checks it and the reaction plan if it goes out of limits.

Key takeaway: Every check needs a reaction plan.

Day 64 · Control Phase

Control Chart

Separate normal variation from real signals.

A control chart plots data over time with a centre line and control limits calculated from the process itself. Points outside the limits or unusual patterns signal a special cause. Points within the limits show normal variation that should not be chased.

Key takeaway: React to signals, not to every point.

Day 65 · Control Phase

SOP Standardization

A good SOP is the best known way, written simply.

Standard operating procedures describe the current best method, with key points and reasons. Use pictures, keep steps short and train people at the workplace. Update the SOP whenever an improvement is proven.

Key takeaway: Standardize the best way, then improve it.

Day 66 · Control Phase

Visual Management

See the status of work without asking.

Visual management makes the status of work, standards and problems visible to everyone at the place of work, so teams can see at a glance whether they are on track.

Key takeaway: Make it visible, then make it better.

Day 67 · Control Phase

OEE Monitoring

Track OEE daily to keep gains visible.

In the Control phase, OEE is monitored by shift on a simple board, with the top losses listed. Trends show whether improvements hold. A drop triggers a quick review and action before losses grow.

Key takeaway: What is watched daily is kept.

Day 68 · Control Phase

Control Phase Failure & Building Continuous Improvement Culture

Without control, results drift back.

Control fails when standards are not updated, owners are not named or monitoring stops after the project. A continuous improvement culture builds daily reviews, audits and recognition so improvement becomes routine work.

Key takeaway: Improvement is a habit, not a project.

Day 69 · Control Phase

Control Plan Example

An example control plan for one step.

A control plan example lists, for a key step, what is measured, the specification, method, frequency, owner and reaction plan. Illustrative case for learning; replace with your own measured data.

Key takeaway: Every control needs a reaction plan.

Day 70 · Advanced Statistics Basics

Mean, Median, Mode & Standard Deviation

Three centres and one spread.

The mean is the average, the median is the middle value and the mode is the most frequent value. Standard deviation measures the typical distance of values from the mean. A small standard deviation means a consistent process.

Key takeaway: Know the centre and the spread.

Day 71 · Advanced Statistics Basics

Normal Distribution

The bell curve describes many process outputs.

In a normal distribution, about 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two and 99.7% within three. Many measurements, such as fill weight, follow this pattern when the process is stable.

Key takeaway: Stable processes often form a bell curve.

Day 72 · Advanced Statistics Basics

Variation Interpretation

Read variation in context before acting.

Look at spread, shape and trend together. A wide spread suggests common cause variation; a sudden shift suggests a special cause; a slow drift may mean wear. Always compare the variation with the customer's specification.

Key takeaway: Interpret before you react.

Day 73 · Advanced Statistics Basics

Statistics in a Real Plant: Example

A filling line records 30 bottle weights per shift. The mean is on target, but the standard deviation is high on one filling head. Fixing that head reduces spread, so the line can run closer to target and give away less product.

Key takeaway: Stats point to where the problem lives.

Day 74 · Advanced Statistics Basics

Misreading Data & Data Driven Leadership

Averages can hide problems.

Common mistakes are using averages alone, mixing data from different machines and drawing conclusions from small samples. Data-driven leaders ask how data was collected, look at charts over time and base decisions on facts.

Key takeaway: Ask how the number was made.

Day 75 · Advanced Statistics Basics

Run Chart & Stratification

See trends over time and split data to find patterns.

A run chart plots data in time order to show trends, shifts and cycles. Stratification splits data by machine, shift, product or supplier to reveal where problems come from. Used together, they quickly narrow the search.

Key takeaway: Plot it over time, then split it.

Day 76 · Process Capability

Cp & Cpk Concept

Cp shows potential; Cpk shows actual performance.

Cp compares the specification width with six standard deviations of the process. Cpk also considers where the process mean sits, using the distance to the nearest limit. If Cp is good but Cpk is poor, the process needs centring.

Key takeaway: Centre the process to turn Cp into Cpk.

Day 77 · Process Capability

USL/LSL

Specification limits come from the customer.

The upper specification limit (USL) and lower specification limit (LSL) define the acceptable range for a characteristic. They are different from control limits, which come from process data. A capable process stays well inside both specification limits.

Key takeaway: Spec limits are the voice of the customer.

Day 78 · Process Capability

Sigma Level Link

A common scale for process performance.

Sigma level is a way to express how many standard deviations fit between the process mean and the nearest specification limit. A higher sigma level means fewer defects; it is a summary, not the goal itself.

Key takeaway: Higher sigma, fewer defects, but keep the customer measure in view.

Day 79 · Process Capability

Process Capability Case Study

A fill weight process has Cp of 1.5 but Cpk of 0.8 because the mean runs high. Adjusting the set point centres the process, raising Cpk to about 1.4 and cutting overfill give-away. No new machine was needed.

Key takeaway: Often centring is the cheapest fix.

Day 80 · Process Capability

Capability Misconceptions & Capability Strategy

Capability means nothing for an unstable process.

Common misconceptions are calculating Cpk on unstable data, using too few samples and ignoring measurement error. A capability strategy checks stability first, verifies the measurement system and reviews capability for critical characteristics regularly.

Key takeaway: Stable first, capable second.

Day 81 · Process Capability

DPU, Yield & First Pass Yield

Count defects per unit and good output first time.

Defects per unit (DPU) is total defects divided by units. Yield is the share of good output. First pass yield counts only units that pass the first time without rework. A low first pass yield shows hidden rework.

Key takeaway: Rework hides inside final yield.

Day 82 · OEE Deep Dive

OEE Formula

Put the three factors together.

OEE is calculated by multiplying availability, performance and quality. Each factor is a percentage of the ideal, so OEE shows overall effectiveness in one number.

Key takeaway: Improve the factor with the biggest loss first.

Day 83 · OEE Deep Dive

Availability Loss

Was the machine running when it should be?

Availability is the share of planned production time that the equipment actually runs. It is reduced by downtime such as breakdowns, changeovers and waiting for material.

Key takeaway: Every unplanned stop reduces availability.

Day 84 · OEE Deep Dive

Performance Loss

Did it run as fast as it should?

Performance compares actual output with what the equipment could produce at its ideal cycle time during run time. It is reduced by slow running and minor stops.

Key takeaway: Small stops and slow speed hide here.

Day 85 · OEE Deep Dive

Quality Loss

How much of the output was good?

The quality rate in OEE is good units divided by total units produced. It is reduced by scrap, rework and start-up rejects.

Key takeaway: Count rework as a loss, not as good output.

Day 86 · OEE Deep Dive

OEE Case

Use OEE losses to choose what to fix.

A packaging line OEE case breaks OEE into availability, performance and quality losses and targets the largest one first. Illustrative case for learning; replace with your own measured data.

Key takeaway: Fix the biggest loss first.

Day 87 · OEE Deep Dive

Fake OEE Trap & OEE Governance

A high OEE number can be false.

OEE is faked by shrinking planned time, using a slower ideal cycle or ignoring small stops. Good OEE governance fixes the definitions, audits data and uses OEE to find losses, not to judge people.

Key takeaway: Honest OEE is more useful than high OEE.

Day 88 · OEE Deep Dive

Andon

A signal that calls for help immediately.

Andon is a visual and sometimes audible signal used to show that a process needs help, such as a light, a board or a cord. It allows problems to be raised and responded to fast.

Key takeaway: Raise the problem fast; respond faster.

Day 89 · 16 Losses

6 Big Losses

The losses that reduce equipment effectiveness.

The six big losses are breakdowns, setup and adjustments (availability losses), minor stops and reduced speed (performance losses), and start-up rejects and production rejects (quality losses).

Key takeaway: Name the loss before trying to fix it.

Day 90 · 16 Losses

Equipment Failures

Breakdowns are the largest availability loss in many plants.

Equipment failure losses are the time a machine cannot run because of a breakdown. Record each stop with time and cause, analyse repeats and act on root causes such as poor lubrication, loose parts or wrong operation.

Key takeaway: Record every breakdown; fix the repeat ones.
