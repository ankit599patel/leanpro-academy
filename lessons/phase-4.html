Losses, SMED, VSM & Flow

Free daily lessons, days 91–120.

Day 91 · 16 Losses

Minor Stops, Startup & Speed Loss

Small stops and slow running add up.

Minor stops are short interruptions, such as jams or sensor faults, that are often not recorded. Startup losses occur before the process stabilizes, and speed losses occur when the line runs below its ideal rate. Together they can be larger than breakdowns.

Key takeaway: Measure the small losses; they are rarely small.

Day 92 · 16 Losses

Six Big Losses Case Study

A packing line records stops for two weeks. Minor stops on the capper cause 9% loss, more than all breakdowns. Fixing the bottle guide and sensor position removes most jams and raises OEE by several points.

Key takeaway: Data reveals which loss is really biggest.

Day 93 · 16 Losses

Hidden Losses & Loss Elimination Strategy

Some losses never appear in reports.

Hidden losses include waiting for material, quality checks, cleaning and operator shortages. A loss elimination strategy maps all losses from total time to good output, ranks them and assigns owners to the largest.

Key takeaway: Map every minute from planned time to good output.

Day 94 · 16 Losses

Condition Monitoring: Vibration & Thermography

Detect problems before they cause breakdowns.

Vibration analysis finds imbalance, misalignment and bearing wear in rotating equipment. Thermography uses infrared images to find hot spots in motors, panels and bearings. Both allow repairs to be planned before failure.

Key takeaway: Listen and look before it fails.

Day 95 · SMED Advanced

Changeover Mapping

Map every step of a changeover.

Record the changeover on video, list each activity with its time and mark who does it. This map shows the real duration, waiting, searching and adjustments, and is the baseline for SMED.

Key takeaway: You cannot reduce what you have not mapped.

Day 96 · SMED Advanced

Internal vs External

What must happen while stopped, and what need not.

Internal setup tasks can only be done while the machine is stopped, such as changing a nozzle. External setup tasks can be done while it is running, such as fetching parts, tools and documents.

Key takeaway: Move every possible task outside the stop.

Day 97 · SMED Advanced

Parallel Activities & Quick Fixing Systems

Do steps in parallel and use quick fasteners.

Two people working in parallel can cut changeover time, as long as tasks are planned and safe. Quick fixing systems such as clamps, quarter-turn fasteners and locating pins replace bolts and reduce adjustment.

Key takeaway: Fewer turns, fewer adjustments, faster change.

Day 98 · SMED Advanced

SMED Practical Example

Apply SMED to a real changeover.

A changeover reduction case films the current changeover, separates internal and external tasks, converts and streamlines them, then standardizes. Illustrative case for learning; replace with your own measured data.

Key takeaway: Film it, separate it, streamline it.

Day 99 · SMED Advanced

SMED Failure Reasons & Strategic Impact

SMED fails when it is not standardized.

SMED improvements are lost when there is no changeover standard, tools are not kept ready or training is skipped. Strategically, faster changeovers allow smaller batches, shorter lead times and more flexible response to customers.

Key takeaway: Short changeovers make small batches possible.

Day 100 · SMED Advanced

Shadow Boards & Point-of-Use Storage for Changeover

Keep changeover tools and parts ready at the point of use.

Shadow boards show where each tool lives, so a missing tool is noticed at once. Point-of-use storage places tools, parts and materials next to where they are used, which removes searching and walking during changeovers.

Key takeaway: A place for everything, next to where it is used.

Day 101 · Advanced VSM

Current State Analysis

Draw the stream as it really is today.

A current state map shows how the value stream works now, based on direct observation: process steps, data boxes, inventory, information flows and a timeline of lead time versus processing time.

Key takeaway: Walk it and draw it as it is, not as it should be.

Day 102 · Advanced VSM

Lead Time Reduction

Lead time shrinks when waiting shrinks.

Most lead time is waiting between process steps, not working time. Reduce it by cutting batch sizes, inventory between steps and queues, and by linking steps with flow or pull.

Key takeaway: Attack the waiting, not just the work.

Day 103 · Advanced VSM

Future State Design

Design how the stream should flow.

A future state map shows how the value stream should work after improvement, using Lean principles: produce to takt, create flow where possible, use pull where flow is not possible, and schedule at one point.

Key takeaway: Design the future, then plan the steps to get there.

Day 104 · Advanced VSM

Flow vs Batch

Small batches flow faster than big ones.

In batch production, each item waits for the whole batch. In flow, items move one or a few at a time, so lead time, inventory and the risk of defects reaching many units all fall.

Key takeaway: Make one, move one, wherever you can.

Day 105 · Advanced VSM

VSM Case Study

An illustrative value stream improvement.

A VSM case study shows the current state, the future state design and the actions taken. This example is illustrative; use your own measured data for real cases.

Key takeaway: Current state, future state, action plan, review.

Day 106 · Advanced VSM

VSM Errors & Value Stream Strategy

A VSM without action is only a drawing.

Common VSM errors are mapping a value stream too wide, using guessed data and never implementing the future state. A value stream strategy turns the future state into a dated action plan with owners and regular reviews.

Key takeaway: Map, plan, act, review.

Day 107 · Advanced VSM

Pull System: Kanban, Supermarket & FIFO Lane

Produce only what the next process takes.

In a pull system, Kanban cards or signals tell the previous process what to replace. A supermarket is a controlled store between processes, and a FIFO lane keeps a fixed, small queue moving in order. Together they limit inventory and keep flow.

Key takeaway: Let actual use trigger production.

Day 108 · Line Balancing

Work Study

Study the method before timing it.

Work study combines method study, which improves how work is done, and work measurement, which sets the time it should take. Improve the method first, otherwise you are timing waste.

Key takeaway: Fix the method, then measure the time.

Day 109 · Line Balancing

Time Study

Measure how long the work really takes.

A time study observes and times a task over several cycles, breaks it into elements, and records the repeatable time for each element, along with any irregular events.

Key takeaway: Observe several cycles; record what really happens.

Day 110 · Line Balancing

Takt Time

The pace the customer demands.

Takt time is the rate at which products must be completed to meet customer demand. Takt time = available production time ÷ customer demand for that period.

Key takeaway: Takt is set by demand, not by the machine.

Day 111 · Line Balancing

Bottleneck Removal

The step that limits the whole process.

A bottleneck is the process step with the lowest capacity relative to demand. It sets the output of the whole system; improving other steps does not increase output.

Key takeaway: An hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the whole line.

Day 112 · Line Balancing

Line Balancing Case Study

A five-station packing line has one station at 52 seconds against a takt of 45 seconds. Moving one element to a station at 30 seconds and removing a walking step brings all stations under takt and raises output without extra people.

Key takeaway: Balance the work to the takt.

Day 113 · Line Balancing

Line Imbalance Mistakes & Throughput Strategy

An unbalanced line runs at the speed of its slowest station.

Common mistakes are balancing on averages, ignoring variation and not updating the balance when demand changes. A throughput strategy finds the constraint, improves it first and balances the other stations around it.

Key takeaway: Improve the constraint first.

Day 114 · Line Balancing

Yamazumi Chart & Work Combination Table

See the workload by station and person.

A Yamazumi chart stacks work elements for each station or operator against the takt time, showing overload and idle time at a glance. A work combination table shows manual, machine and walking time for one operator over a cycle.

Key takeaway: Make the workload visible, then rebalance.

Day 115 · Inventory Optimization

Inventory Types

Three types of inventory along the stream.

Raw material is purchased input not yet used. Work in progress (WIP) is partly processed material. Finished goods (FG) are completed products ready for sale. Each type has different causes and controls.

Key takeaway: Know which type of stock is growing, and why.

Day 116 · Inventory Optimization

EOQ Concept

Balance ordering cost against holding cost.

Economic order quantity (EOQ) is a classic model for the order size that minimizes the total of ordering and holding costs under steady demand: EOQ = √(2 × annual demand × cost per order ÷ holding cost per unit per year).

Key takeaway: Use EOQ as a guide, then apply real constraints.

Day 117 · Inventory Optimization

Safety Stock

A buffer for uncertainty.

Safety stock is extra inventory held to protect against variation in demand and supply lead time. Its size depends on how much variation exists and the service level wanted.

Key takeaway: Reduce variation to reduce safety stock.

Day 118 · Inventory Optimization

Inventory Turnover

Turnover shows how fast inventory moves.

Inventory turnover is annual cost of goods used divided by average inventory value. Higher turnover means less cash tied up and less risk of expiry. Track it by category, not only for the whole plant.

Key takeaway: Stock that does not move costs money.

Day 119 · Inventory Optimization

Inventory Case Example

An illustrative case of excess stock.

An inventory waste case shows how excess stock built up, what it cost and how it was reduced. This example is illustrative; use your own data for real cases.

Key takeaway: Cheap to buy can be expensive to hold.

Day 120 · Inventory Optimization

Overstocking Trap & Lean Inventory Strategy

Extra stock hides problems and ties up cash.

Overstocking happens through large minimum orders, fear of shortage and poor forecasts. A Lean inventory strategy sets limits by item, uses pull and Kanban for regular items and fixes the causes of uncertainty instead of buffering them.

Key takeaway: Lower the water to see the rocks.
