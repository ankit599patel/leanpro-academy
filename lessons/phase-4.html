Day 91 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

What Is Six Sigma?

Reduce variation so every output meets the requirement.

Six Sigma is a data-driven approach to improving processes by reducing variation and defects. It uses the DMAIC method and statistical tools to find and control the causes of variation.

Key takeaway: Less variation means fewer defects.

Day 92 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

What Is Lean Six Sigma?

Lean speed plus Six Sigma precision.

Lean Six Sigma combines Lean's focus on removing waste and improving flow with Six Sigma's focus on reducing variation and defects. Together they make processes faster and more reliable.

Key takeaway: Remove waste and reduce variation together.

Day 93 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Lean vs Six Sigma

Two approaches with different strengths.

Lean targets waste and lead time using tools like VSM, 5S and kanban. Six Sigma targets variation and defects using data and statistics through DMAIC. Choose by the type of problem.

Key takeaway: Waste problem? Lean. Variation problem? Six Sigma.

Day 94 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Variation Basics

No two outputs are exactly the same.

Variation is the natural difference between outputs of the same process. Common-cause variation is built into the process; special-cause variation comes from a specific, unusual event and should be investigated.

Key takeaway: Know which variation you are looking at before acting.

Day 95 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Process Capability

Can the process meet the specification every time?

Process capability compares the natural spread of a stable process with the specification limits. Indices such as Cp and Cpk express how well the process fits within the limits and how centred it is.

Key takeaway: A process must be stable before capability means anything.

Day 96 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Defects per Unit

Average number of defects found per unit.

Defects per unit (DPU) is the total number of defects found divided by the number of units inspected. One unit can have more than one defect.

Key takeaway: Count every defect, not just defective units.

Day 97 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

DPMO

Defects per million opportunities.

DPMO expresses defects relative to the number of opportunities for a defect. DPMO = defects ÷ (units × opportunities per unit) × 1,000,000. It allows comparison of processes with different complexity.

Key takeaway: Define the opportunities clearly before calculating.

Day 98 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Yield

How many units come out right.

Yield is the proportion of units that pass a process step without defects. First-pass yield counts only units right the first time; rolled throughput yield multiplies first-pass yields across all steps.

Key takeaway: Small losses at each step multiply across the process.

Day 99 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Sigma Level Concept

A common scale for process performance.

Sigma level is a way to express how many standard deviations fit between the process mean and the nearest specification limit. A higher sigma level means fewer defects; it is a summary, not the goal itself.

Key takeaway: Higher sigma, fewer defects, but keep the customer measure in view.

Day 100 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

DMAIC Overview

Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control.

DMAIC is the five-phase Six Sigma method for improving an existing process: Define the problem, Measure the current performance, Analyze causes, Improve with tested solutions, and Control to sustain the gain.

Key takeaway: Follow the phases; do not skip to solutions.

Day 101 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Define Phase

Agree the problem, scope and goal first.

The Define phase clarifies the problem, the customer and their requirements, the project scope, the goal and the team. Key outputs are a project charter, a SIPOC and the voice of the customer.

Key takeaway: A well-defined problem is half solved.

Day 102 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Voice of Customer

What the customer says they need, in their words.

Voice of the Customer (VOC) is the collection of customer needs, expectations and complaints, gathered through interviews, surveys, complaints and feedback, and translated into measurable requirements.

Key takeaway: Listen first, then translate into measures.

Day 103 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

CTQ Tree

From a broad need to measurable requirements.

A CTQ (critical-to-quality) tree breaks a broad customer need into specific drivers and then into measurable requirements with targets or limits.

Key takeaway: Every CTQ must be measurable.

Day 104 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

SIPOC

Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers.

SIPOC is a high-level map of a process that lists Suppliers, Inputs, the main Process steps, Outputs and Customers. It sets the project boundaries and shows who is involved.

Key takeaway: Agree the boundaries before diving into details.

Day 105 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Project Charter

The agreement that launches a project.

A project charter documents the business case, problem statement, goal, scope, team, timeline and sponsor. It aligns the team and management before work begins.

Key takeaway: No charter, no shared direction.

Day 106 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Measure Phase

Understand current performance with reliable data.

The Measure phase establishes how the process performs today. The team defines measures, checks that the measurement system is reliable, collects baseline data and maps the process in detail.

Key takeaway: Trust the measurement before you trust the data.

Day 107 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Operational Definition

Everyone measures the same thing the same way.

An operational definition describes exactly what to measure and how, including the method, the unit, the conditions and what counts as a defect, so that different people get the same result.

Key takeaway: Define it so two people get the same answer.

Day 108 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Data Collection Plan

Plan what, how, when and who before collecting.

A data collection plan specifies what data to collect, the operational definitions, the sample size and frequency, who collects it, how it is recorded and how it will be used.

Key takeaway: Plan the data before you collect it.

Day 109 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Measurement System

Is the measurement itself reliable?

A measurement system analysis (MSA) checks how much of the observed variation comes from the measurement system itself, through studies of repeatability, reproducibility, bias and stability.

Key takeaway: Fix the gauge before blaming the process.

Day 110 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Analyze Phase

Find and verify the causes that drive the problem.

The Analyze phase identifies possible causes, then uses data and tests to verify which ones actually drive the problem. Tools include fishbone, 5 Whys, Pareto, scatter plots and hypothesis tests.

Key takeaway: Verify causes with data before improving.

Day 111 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Cause-and-Effect Matrix

Score which inputs matter most to the CTQs.

A cause-and-effect (C&E) matrix rates how strongly each process input affects each customer requirement, weighted by importance, to prioritize which inputs to study first.

Key takeaway: Prioritize inputs by impact on what the customer needs.

Day 112 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Improve Phase

Develop, test and prove solutions.

The Improve phase generates solutions for the verified root causes, selects the best ones, tests them in a pilot and confirms with data that the problem is reduced.

Key takeaway: Pilot first, then scale what works.

Day 113 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Solution Selection

Choose solutions with clear criteria.

Solution selection compares options against agreed criteria, such as effect on the root cause, cost, time, risk and ease of implementation, often using a simple weighted matrix.

Key takeaway: Decide with criteria, not with opinions.

Day 114 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Pilot Run

Test on a small scale before full rollout.

A pilot run tests a solution under real conditions on a limited scale, such as one line or one shift, to confirm it works, find side effects and refine it before full implementation.

Key takeaway: Small pilots prevent big mistakes.

Day 115 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Control Phase

Hold the gain after the project ends.

The Control phase makes sure improvements last, using standard work, control plans, control charts, mistake-proofing and clear ownership, before the project is handed over to the process owner.

Key takeaway: If it is not controlled, it will drift back.

Day 116 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Control Plan

One document for how the process stays in control.

A control plan lists, for each key process step, what is measured, the specification, the method, the sample size and frequency, who checks it and the reaction plan if it goes out of limits.

Key takeaway: Every check needs a reaction plan.

Day 117 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Control Charts Intro

See whether variation is normal or special.

A control chart plots data over time with a centre line and control limits calculated from the process itself. Points outside the limits or unusual patterns signal special causes to investigate.

Key takeaway: React to signals, not to every point.

Day 118 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Six Sigma Roles

Who does what in a Six Sigma program.

Six Sigma programs define roles: a Champion or sponsor removes barriers, Master Black Belts coach, Black Belts lead complex projects, Green Belts lead smaller projects, and team members contribute process knowledge.

Key takeaway: Clear roles make projects move.

Day 119 · Six Sigma & DMAIC

Green Belt vs Black Belt

Two levels of Six Sigma practitioner.

Green Belts usually lead smaller projects part-time within their own area. Black Belts typically lead larger, cross-functional projects, use more advanced statistics and coach Green Belts. Exact requirements vary by training body.

Key takeaway: Choose the level that matches the project scope.
